The basics

What does a speech and language evaluation actually include?

From the Well Spoken library · 8 min read · Reviewed by a licensed speech-language pathologist

If you have never been through one, "speech evaluation" sounds vague and a little clinical, which makes it easy to put off. So here is the whole thing, step by step, with nothing mysterious left in.

Before the appointment: the intake

A good evaluation starts before anyone meets your child. You will fill out a case history covering pregnancy and birth, developmental milestones, medical history including ear infections and hearing, family history of speech or language issues, and what languages your child hears at home. Then you will describe your concern in your own words. This part matters more than parents expect. The clinician is forming hypotheses from your description before the first toy comes out.

The appointment itself

Plan for one to two hours, sometimes split across two visits for young children. It rarely looks like a test. For a preschooler it looks like playing on the floor with someone who happens to be very organized about which toys come out and what questions get asked. A typical evaluation weaves together several strands:

  • Standardized testing. Structured activities with pictures, objects, and questions, scored against large samples of same-age children. This is where standard scores and percentiles come from.
  • A language sample. The clinician engineers a conversation or play scenario and quietly tracks the length of your child's sentences, the grammar they use, and how they put a story together. Often the most revealing part.
  • A speech sound inventory. Which sounds your child can produce, which they substitute or drop, and whether the pattern fits their age.
  • An oral mechanism check. A quick look at lips, tongue, teeth, and palate to rule out structural explanations.
  • Observation of everything else. How your child engages, asks for help, handles frustration, and uses gestures and eye contact. The skilled clinician is collecting data the entire time, including in the waiting room.

You may be in the room, especially with toddlers, or observing. Either way, expect to be interviewed too. Your report of what your child does at home is treated as real data, because it is.

After: scoring, the report, and the conversation

The clinician scores the tests, integrates everything, and writes a report. A thorough one includes background, what was administered, standardized scores with plain-language explanations, clinical observations, a summary of strengths as well as concerns, a clear statement of whether therapy or further referral is recommended, and specific goals if it is. We wrote a separate guide to decoding that report.

Then comes the part that separates good evaluations from paperwork: a real conversation. You should leave understanding what was found, what it means, what happens next, and what you can do at home. If you do not get that conversation, ask for it. You are allowed.

How this differs from a school screening

A screening, the kind sometimes done in preschools or kindergarten, is a 15 to 30 minute pass/refer check. It answers one question: should this child be looked at more closely? It produces no scores, no diagnosis, and no plan. A full evaluation is the closer look itself. If your child "passed a screening" but your concern persists, those two facts can both be true, and the screening should not end the conversation. School-based evaluations are a separate topic with their own rules, covered in private vs. school evaluations.

The takeaway

An evaluation is not a verdict on your child or your parenting. It is a few hours of careful looking by someone trained to see patterns, ending in a conversation that replaces guessing with a plan. Of everything on this site, it is the step parents most consistently describe afterward with the word "relief."
