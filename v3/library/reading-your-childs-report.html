After the eval

Reading your child's evaluation report: a guide for parents

From the Well Spoken library · 8 min read · Reviewed by a licensed speech-language pathologist

You waited weeks for the evaluation, and now you are holding a report that reads like it was written for someone else. In fairness, parts of it were: reports also speak to schools, insurers, and other clinicians. This guide translates the parts that matter.

Standard scores: the number doing the most work

Most language tests use standard scores where 100 is the exact average and most children score between 85 and 115. That range, one standard deviation each way, covers about two thirds of all children, and scores inside it are generally considered within normal limits.

  • 85 to 115: within the average range. A 92 is not a "low" score, even though it is under 100.
  • 78 to 85 or so: a gray zone. Below average, possibly meaningful, and the clinical observations matter most here.
  • Below 78: most clinicians and many school districts treat this as a significant difference worth addressing.

Percentiles say the same thing another way: the percentage of same-age children scoring at or below your child. The 50th percentile is exactly average. The 16th percentile corresponds to a standard score around 85. One thing worth knowing: percentiles bunch up near the middle, so the gap between the 50th and 37th percentile is tiny in practice, while the gap between the 16th and the 3rd is large.

The words around the numbers

  • Receptive language: what your child understands. Expressive language: what they produce. A gap between the two is a finding in itself.
  • Articulation or phonology: speech sounds. Reports often list error patterns and whether they are age-appropriate, since some sound errors are entirely normal at certain ages.
  • "Within normal limits": genuinely fine on that measure. Not code for anything.
  • Clinical impressions: the clinician's professional judgment, weaving scores together with everything they observed. In a good report, this section tells the story; read it twice.

What a strong report contains, and what a thin one is missing

A strong report has: your child's background and the reason for referral, the specific tests given, scores with plain-language explanations, observational findings, strengths alongside concerns, a clear recommendation, and, if therapy is recommended, specific goals. A thin report has scores with no interpretation, vague impressions like "mild delay" with nothing behind them, or no recommendation at all. A thin report is a reason to ask questions, and sometimes a reason to consider a second opinion.

Red flags vs. normal variability

One below-average subtest score in an otherwise average profile is usually noise, not signal. Children have off days, and tests have measurement error. What deserves attention is a pattern: multiple scores trending low in the same domain, a wide receptive-expressive gap, scores that contradict what you see at home (in either direction), or numbers that disagree with the written impressions without explanation.

Questions worth bringing back to the evaluator

  • Which finding worried you most, and which least?
  • Is this profile something children typically grow through, or does it usually need support?
  • What would you do if this were your child?
  • What specifically should we do at home?
  • If we do nothing for six months, what do you expect to happen?

A good evaluator welcomes every one of these. The report is not the end of the evaluation. The conversation is, and you are entitled to it.
