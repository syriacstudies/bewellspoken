Getting ready

How to prepare for your child's speech evaluation

From the Well Spoken library · 5 min read · Reviewed by a licensed speech-language pathologist

You booked the evaluation. The anxiety that follows is usually about the unknown, so here is everything you can actually do, and the short list of things you should deliberately not do.

What to gather beforehand

  • Prior reports, if any exist: earlier evaluations, Early Intervention paperwork, IEPs or 504 plans, hearing test results.
  • A recent hearing check, or be ready to say when the last one was. Hearing is the first thing a careful clinician rules out, and frequent ear infections are worth mentioning.
  • Your own notes. When you first noticed the concern, examples of how your child says things (write them phonetically, like "I tan do it"), words they use, and what a hard moment looks like. If you took our check-in, bring the printed summary. This is exactly what it is for.
  • A short video, if your child clams up around strangers. Thirty seconds of them chatting at dinner gives the clinician evidence of what your child can do when comfortable. Clinicians genuinely use these.
  • The languages picture, if your home is multilingual: which languages your child hears, from whom, and roughly how much. Differences from hearing two languages are not disorders, and a good evaluator needs this context to tell the two apart.

Setting your child up for an honest showing

The goal is not a best-behavior performance. It is a typical day, observed carefully.

  • Schedule around naps and meals as much as the clinic allows. A hungry two year old tests as a hungrier version of themselves.
  • Describe it honestly and lightly: "We are going to play with someone whose job is playing and talking with kids." No rehearsals, no stakes.
  • Bring the comfort object, a snack for after, and low expectations about the waiting room.
  • If your child has a meltdown or refuses to talk, do not panic. Clinicians see this weekly, and how a child warms up, or does not, is itself useful information.

What not to coach

This is the counterintuitive part: do not practice. Do not drill sounds in the car, do not prompt "say it the right way" that morning, and during the session resist the urge to answer for your child, repeat the clinician's questions, or whisper hints. A coached performance hides exactly what the evaluator needs to see, and an inflated result can delay help your child would benefit from. The evaluation has no pass or fail. The only bad outcome is an inaccurate picture.

What you will be doing

Expect to be interviewed, sometimes for a substantial chunk of the appointment. Answer plainly, including about the things your child does well. If you are observing the testing, your job is pleasant wallpaper: warm, quiet, and letting silences sit so your child fills them instead of you.

Afterward

Before you leave, ask when the report will arrive and whether a conversation to walk through it is included. It should be. Then read the report with our plain-language guide open next to it, and write down your questions while they are fresh.
