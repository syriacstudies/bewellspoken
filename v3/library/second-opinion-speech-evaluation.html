When it feels off

When to seek a second opinion on a speech evaluation

From the Well Spoken library · 6 min read · Reviewed by a licensed speech-language pathologist

You got the evaluation. You read the report. And something about it sits wrong. Maybe the conclusion does not match the child you live with, or the report feels thinner than the concern deserved. Second opinions are normal in every corner of healthcare, and speech and language is no exception. Here is how to think about it without spiraling.

First, separate two different problems

Problem one: the evaluation seems incomplete. This is about craft. Signs of a thin evaluation include a report with scores but no interpretation, no language sample or observational findings for a young child, vague impressions like "mild delay" with nothing specific behind them, no recommendations section, or an appointment so short it could not have covered much. Thin work is a quality issue, and a re-evaluation is a reasonable response.

Problem two: the evaluation seems solid, but the conclusion surprises you. This one deserves more care, because a careful evaluation that contradicts your expectation might be wrong, or might be the most useful information you have received yet. Before seeking a second opinion here, go back to the first evaluator with your specific disagreement: "You found X, but at home we consistently see Y. Can you help me square those?" Good clinicians engage with that question seriously, and the answer often resolves things without another evaluation.

When a second opinion is clearly worth it

  • "Does not qualify" from a school evaluation, while your concern persists. The most common case. School evaluations answer an eligibility question, not the whole question; we explain the difference in private vs. school evaluations. A child can fail to qualify for school services and still benefit from private support.
  • The findings and your daily reality genuinely conflict, and the conversation with the first evaluator did not resolve it.
  • The report is thin in the ways described above.
  • Six months have passed, you followed the plan, and nothing has moved. Whether the original answer was "fine, keep watching" or a therapy plan that is not producing change, new data is reasonable.
  • The stakes of the decision are high: the results will drive school placement, a diagnosis, or an expensive course of therapy.

When it is probably not worth it

Also honesty: a second opinion is not the move when the first report was thorough and the conclusion is simply hard to hear, when you are hoping a different evaluator will say the worry away, or when the disagreement is about a borderline score that both evaluators would call a judgment call. Re-evaluating in pursuit of a preferred answer costs money and time and tends to end with two reports that say nearly the same thing.

How to do it well

  • Tell the second evaluator a first evaluation exists, and share it. Hiding it wastes their time and yours; a good clinician will form their own view anyway.
  • Ask the practice beforehand: who will do the testing, how long the appointment is, what it covers, and whether a parent conversation about the results is included.
  • Mind the timing. Some standardized tests cannot be validly repeated within a short window. Mention which tests were used so the second evaluator can choose differently if needed.
  • Bring your own organized observations. The check-in summary works for this too.

One more reassurance: asking for a second opinion does not insult the first clinician. It is your child, your call, and any professional worth seeing again knows that.
