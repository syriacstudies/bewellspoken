Private vs. school speech evaluation: when and why

From the Well Spoken library · 7 min read · Reviewed by a licensed speech-language pathologist

This is an honest comparison, not a pitch. Both paths involve qualified speech-language pathologists. They answer different questions, they cost different amounts, and for many families the right answer is one then the other, or both.

What schools are required to do

In the United States, public schools must evaluate children suspected of having a disability that affects their education, at no cost to you, under IDEA. This includes preschoolers from age three, even before kindergarten, and children under three are covered by Early Intervention programs. You can request an evaluation in writing at any time, and the school must respond within defined timelines.

A school evaluation is free, conducted by licensed professionals, and is the only path to school-based services like an IEP. Those are real strengths and they should not be talked down.

The catch: the question schools are answering

Here is the part parents are rarely told plainly. A school evaluation does not ask "does this child have a speech or language issue?" It asks "does this child's issue adversely affect their education enough to qualify for services here?" Those are different questions. A child can have a real, treatable articulation issue or a mild language delay and still not qualify, because they are passing their classes. "Does not qualify" gets heard as "nothing is wrong," and that is not what it means.

School evaluations can also be shaped by caseload realities and by eligibility cutoffs that vary by district. None of this makes school SLPs less skilled. It makes the institutional question narrower than your question, which is simply "what is going on with my kid?"

What a private evaluation offers

  • Your question, answered directly. A private evaluation assesses the whole communication picture, not eligibility.
  • Speed. School timelines can legally run two months or more. Private evaluations are often scheduled within weeks.
  • Depth and specificity. More time per child, broader test batteries when warranted, and a report written for you rather than for an eligibility committee.
  • A second data point. A private report can be brought into the school process. Schools must consider outside evaluations, even though they are not required to adopt their conclusions.

The honest downside: cost. Private evaluations typically run several hundred to over a thousand dollars depending on scope and region. Insurance sometimes covers part with a physician referral. Always ask the practice and your insurer beforehand.

A reasonable way to decide

  • Money is tight, or services at school are the goal: start with the school evaluation. It is free and it is your legal right.
  • You want answers quickly, or your concern is broader than school performance: a private evaluation gets you the fuller picture sooner.
  • The school said "does not qualify" but your concern has not gone away: this is the single most common reason families go private, and it is a good one. See our guide to second opinions.
  • Both: entirely legitimate, and common. They answer different questions.

Whichever path you take

Put requests to schools in writing, keep copies of everything, and read whatever report you receive with our report guide beside it. And if you have not yet organized what you are noticing, the check-in gives either evaluator a head start.
