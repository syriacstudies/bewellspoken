Signs your child may benefit from a speech evaluation

From the Well Spoken library · 7 min read · Reviewed by a licensed speech-language pathologist

Here is the uncomfortable truth about "does my child need speech therapy": there is no bright line. Children develop on wildly different timelines, and a quiet two year old can be a chatty three year old without anyone ever intervening. At the same time, some signs really do deserve attention, and the families who act on them early are almost never sorry they did.

This guide is our attempt at the honest version. Not "every quirk needs therapy," and not "they will grow out of it." The real middle.

The signs that matter at every age

A few things are worth a professional conversation no matter how old your child is:

  • Losing skills. Words, sounds, or social behaviors your child used to have and no longer uses. This is the one sign clinicians never want families to sit on. The explanation is often benign, but it should always be looked at promptly.
  • Frustration around communicating. Regular meltdowns, giving up, or avoiding talking. A child who is struggling to be understood is having a hard time, whatever the underlying cause turns out to be.
  • Your own persistent gut feeling. Research on parent concern is consistent: parents flag real issues more often than they invent them. If the worry keeps coming back, that alone is a valid reason to ask.

By age, roughly

The full picture lives on our milestones page, but here are the headline markers most clinicians agree on.

  • By 12 months: little or no babbling, not responding to their name, no gestures like pointing or waving.
  • By 18 months: fewer than 10 words family members recognize.
  • By 24 months: fewer than 50 words, or no two-word combinations like "more milk."
  • By 3 years: strangers cannot understand most of what your child says, or your child is still mostly using single words.
  • By 4 to 5 years: sentences stay very short or simple, directions with two parts are hard, or stuttering has lasted more than six months or comes with visible struggle.
  • School age: trouble following classroom directions peers manage, difficulty telling a story that hangs together, reading falling behind in a way that surprises everyone, or social conversation that is a clear struggle rather than shyness.

When "wait and see" is actually reasonable

Honesty cuts both ways, so here it is: sometimes waiting is fine. A two year old with 60 words who is adding new ones every week, understanding everything, pointing, playing, and engaged with people is very likely on a typical path even if a cousin the same age talks more. A four year old who still says "wabbit" is within the normal window for the r sound, which can develop as late as age seven.

The pattern that makes waiting reasonable is this: your child is making steady forward progress, understands language well for their age, and is connected and communicative even if the words lag. The pattern that makes waiting risky is a plateau, a regression, or a gap between what your child understands and what they can express that keeps widening.

What acting early actually costs you

Very little, which is the point. An evaluation is a few hours of your time and, if private, a fee that many families find worthwhile for the clarity alone. The possible outcomes are: reassurance from a professional, a few targeted things to do at home, or early support for something real. Speech and language issues respond best to early help, and not one of those three outcomes involves regret.

Compare that with the cost of waiting on something real: months of a child working harder than they need to, and a parent lying awake doing amateur diagnostics at midnight. We built this site because that second cost is the one nobody prices in.

A reasonable next step

If parts of this page sounded like your kid, do not jump to worst cases. Take the check-in, print the summary, and bring it to your pediatrician or a speech-language pathologist. One conversation with the right person will do more than fifty more searches.
